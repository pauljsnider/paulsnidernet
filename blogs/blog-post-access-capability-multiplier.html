Access Is Becoming the New Capability Multiplier

Paul Snider Technology Executive & MBA

OpenAI's capabilities are impressive on their own, but agents get dramatically more useful when they can reach partner ecosystems, APIs, browsers, data, and enterprise systems.

That is what makes the recent Collusion Wiki story so interesting to me. The site describes a pretty wild scenario: thousands of agents allegedly finding a public wiki, sharing information, and discovering ways to work around intended boundaries.

Take the Story With a Grain of Salt

I would not treat every detail as established fact. The site itself describes the findings as preliminary, and some of the attribution is based on inference. There is still a lot we do not know.

And while the story is scary if you take it at face value, it also feels almost too good to be true for everyone involved from a marketing perspective. It showcases extremely capable OpenAI agents while also reinforcing the value of the surrounding tools, infrastructure, partner ecosystems, and systems those agents need access to.

I do not mean that as an accusation. I just think the story lines up almost perfectly with where the market is already moving. Separate the signal from the storytelling.

The signal is hard to ignore. OpenAI separately documented an August cybersecurity evaluation where agents formed unauthorized communication channels and eventually gained internet access. So the underlying idea that capable agents can behave in unexpected ways once they have tools and connectivity deserves to be taken seriously.

The Model Is Only Part of the System

Intelligence is increasingly the starting point. Access is the multiplier.

An agent that can reason but cannot interact with anything is interesting. An agent that can securely interact with a browser, GitHub, CRM systems, internal APIs, data platforms, observability systems, and specialized partner services is something very different.

OpenAI is saying much of the same thing in its enterprise strategy. Its Partner Network announcement argues that model capability is no longer the primary limiting factor for enterprise value. The harder work is integrating with existing systems and data, redesigning workflows, and deploying AI safely at scale.

I think that creates a huge product opportunity. We are going to spend more time thinking about capabilities built not only for humans to use, but also for agents to consume. APIs, tools, data products, workflows, and domain-specific services become building blocks an intelligent system can discover and use to complete real work.

Access Cuts Both Ways

That opportunity comes with real responsibility. The more systems agents can reach, the more important identity, scoped permissions, least privilege, observability, auditability, egress controls, and clear approval boundaries become.

Security is paramount. Not because the answer is to slow down AI, but because broader access dramatically increases both the usefulness of an agent and the consequences when its behavior is not what we expected.

Traditional software security often starts with the paths we designed. Agentic systems force us to think harder about the paths a capable system might discover on its own. If something is reachable, writable, or callable, we should assume an agent may eventually figure out how to use it.

The Point

Things are changing incredibly fast. I am excited by where this is going, and stories like this reinforce just how powerful these systems are becoming.

I am also increasingly convinced that the winners will not simply have the best model. They will have the best ecosystem of capabilities around the model, the cleanest ways for agents to access those capabilities, and the discipline to expose them securely.

Take Collusion Wiki with a grain of salt. But do not ignore the direction of travel.

Access is becoming the new capability multiplier.
