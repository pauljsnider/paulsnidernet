The $30 Billion, Multi-Year Problem

Paul Snider Technology Executive & MBA

Across multiple industries, the country is carrying highly regulated, multi-billion-dollar modernization projects that run for years, sometimes decades. The frontier AI labs and the cloud companies say their technology can change that math. That promise still needs to be realized at this scale, and these projects are where they have a real opportunity to realize it.

Start with the pattern, because the pattern is the story. Air traffic control. Veterans' health records. Core banking and insurance systems. In each case the structure is the same: a system that cannot be allowed to fail, a regulatory burden that is real and necessary, and a modernization program priced in billions of dollars with a timeline measured in years. I have spent my career in healthcare technology and have worked on systems in this category.

Into that pattern, the frontier AI labs and cloud companies are making a specific promise: that AI changes the economics of this work, compressing years into months and billions into millions. It has not yet been realized on a program of this size, and that gap between promise and proof is exactly the opportunity. Two current programs show both.

1. Air traffic control: a $30 billion request, forty years in

On Friday, November 13, the FAA ordered every departing flight in the United States to stay on the ground. The nationwide ground stop ran from 3:54 p.m. to about 8:15 p.m. Eastern, more than four hours. Reuters reported it was only the second nationwide stop of all departures since September 11, 2001. The FAA called the event a "temporary telecommunications outage." As of Friday night, it had not confirmed a root cause. Reporting by CBS News and the Wall Street Journal pointed to a failed update and reboot on the FAA's telecommunications network, operated by L3Harris; the FAA had not confirmed that account and L3Harris had not commented. Officials said there was no indication of a cyberattack and, in the same briefing, that they were not ruling one out. Homeland Security Secretary Markwayne Mullin, at the briefing, described adding new technology to an aging system as "patchwork." By Friday evening, FlightAware was counting roughly 9,000 to 11,000 delays and roughly 4,000 to 6,000 cancellations for the day, counts that include disruption from other causes.

That morning, the White House had renewed a formal request to Congress for $30.1 billion for aviation: $10.1 billion for air traffic control modernization, including $2 billion for cloud computing, plus $2 billion for control towers and $18 billion for airport projects. Requested is not appropriated: the only money actually appropriated for the rebuild as of Friday was $12.5 billion, provided in July 2025. At the evening briefing, Transportation Secretary Sean Duffy raised the figure himself:

The president's asked for $30 billion. I want $50 billion.

The $50 billion is an ask stated at a briefing. No breakdown or scope was published, and reporting does not settle whether it replaces the $30 billion request or sits on top of it. The administration's own program figure for the rebuild, published in December 2025, was $31.5 to $32.5 billion, and in September 2026 the Government Accountability Office reported that the FAA had not developed a reliable lifecycle cost estimate for the program.

  • $12.5B: Appropriated by Congress, July 2025. The only figure that is actual funding.
  • $30.1B: Formally requested by the White House, renewed the morning of Nov 13. Not appropriated.
  • $50B: Secretary Duffy's ask at the Nov 13 briefing. No breakdown or scope published.

The FAA has been working to modernize air traffic control since the 1980s. GAO reported in September 2024 that 51 of its 138 systems were unsustainable and 54 more potentially unsustainable. The copper telecommunications lines date to the 1960s; as of May 2026, 2,560 of 5,170 copper connections had been replaced with fiber. The previous large program, NextGen, began in 2003: the Transportation Department's Inspector General reported in September 2025 that more than $15 billion had been invested through December 2024, with about 16 percent of expected benefits realized. The current rebuild, run by integrator Peraton, targets its first phase by December 2028, and GAO reported in September 2026 that it has no integrated master schedule.

2. The same shape in healthcare: the VA record program

Healthcare has a program with the same structure. In June 2017, the Department of Veterans Affairs decided to replace its long-running record system, VistA, with the same core Cerner Millennium platform the Defense Department uses. In May 2018, VA signed a ten-year contract with Cerner valued at $10 billion, with a ceiling of $9.99 billion. Oracle acquired Cerner in a deal valued at about $28.3 billion that closed in June 2022, and the contract continued under Oracle Health.

The first hospital, in Spokane, Washington, went live in October 2020. VA paused new deployments in April 2023 and resumed them in April 2026. In August 2026, VA raised the ceiling from $9.99 billion to $26.9 billion and extended ordering through 2031. A ceiling is a maximum, not a spend: GAO reports about $13.84 billion obligated from fiscal 2018 through the second quarter of fiscal 2025. Lifecycle estimates vary by scope and author, from $16.1 billion (VA, 2019) to $56.5 billion (GAO's reading of a VA document, September 2026), and GAO's position is that VA has no updated, independent lifecycle estimate.

  • $9.99B to $26.9B: Contract ceiling, May 2018 to August 2026. A ceiling is the most that may be ordered, not money spent.
  • $13.84B: Obligated across the program, fiscal 2018 through Q2 fiscal 2025, per GAO.
  • About 11%: Of VA medical centers live: 19 of roughly 170, as of late October 2026. The total varies by source, so the percentage is approximate.

What is done: 19 medical centers are live, 13 of them in 2026, and VA reported in October 2026 that more than 780,000 veterans and 42,000 clinicians and staff use the system. What remains: the rest of the roughly 170 centers, with four Wisconsin and Michigan hospitals next in January 2027 and completion targeted for 2031. The facts worth carrying forward: a multi-billion-dollar program, a ceiling that has grown, timelines that have moved, and deployment at about 11 percent after eight years.

3. The promise the AI and cloud companies are making

For decades, the binding constraint on this work was human throughput: understanding a legacy system meant paying scarce experts to read the code, reconstruct what it does, translate it, and test the translation. The claim from the frontier labs and cloud companies is that AI attacks that specific constraint.

Anthropic made the boldest version in February 2026: its Claude Code tools can map a COBOL codebase and generate documentation and translation drafts, so teams "can modernize their COBOL codebase in quarters instead of years." AWS reports that its AWS Transform service processed more than 4.5 billion lines of legacy code and saved more than 1.6 million hours in its first year, both vendor-reported figures, with Toyota Motor North America among its reported customer results, converting more than 40 million lines of COBOL to Java. Amazon has also reported an internal result: more than 30,000 Java applications upgraded in a few months, work it estimated at 4,500 developer-years and $260 million a year in savings. OpenAI reported in October 2026 that its newest model migrates COBOL programs with behavior preserved at 97.9 percent accuracy at higher reasoning effort, on OpenAI's own evaluation. Microsoft reports migration effort reductions of up to 70 percent in early reports. SpaceX figures here through connectivity rather than code: Starlink terminals were sent to the FAA for testing in 2025, SpaceX stated there was "no effort or intent" for Starlink to take over any FAA contract, and in August 2026 the FAA made AT&T the lead contractor for the successor network, starting with a $74.3 million task order.

4. What has been demonstrated so far

The demonstrated results are real, and they are bounded. Amazon's Java upgrade is the strongest example: many applications moved to a newer version of the same language, inside one company, against an existing test suite. The UK's Department for Work and Pensions moved more than 25 million lines onto AWS and cut batch time by 60 percent, per its partner's case study, by keeping its COBOL and changing the platform underneath it. The closest federal-scale refactor in the research, an unnamed large U.S. agency refactor by Amdocs on AWS GovCloud, 1,282 COBOL programs with byte-for-byte validation, took 36 months. None of the five companies in this post has an independently verified, end-to-end AI-led migration of a safety-critical air traffic control system or a national health record system. Anthropic's post named no customer, OpenAI states that production use still requires expert review, and AWS's line counts have no published methodology or independent audit. Gartner predicted in April 2026 that more than 70 percent of mainframe exit projects initiated in 2026 will fail to produce their intended benefits, largely from overestimating generative AI tooling. That is a forecast, not a measurement.

5. The opportunity

None of that diminishes the opportunity. It defines it. The pattern above is what happens when programs are very large, timelines very long, and the scarce resource is expert human comprehension of systems built over decades. AI genuinely addresses that resource, and the companies that demonstrate it on regulated, safety-critical work will have established something no benchmark can.

The way to demonstrate it is specific. Take one bounded, safety-critical slice of a system like the ones above, and modernize it under the real constraints: characterization tests, published equivalence testing, a parallel run long enough to surface rare cases, independent verification by people who do not work for the vendor, and a named human accountable for the cutover. Publish the timeline and the cost next to the original estimate. Finish with the legacy component switched off. Then repeat it, larger.

Friday's ground stop will leave the news cycle; the programs behind it will not. A $30 billion request, a $26.9 billion ceiling, timelines running to 2031: that is the standing opportunity, and the invitation is simply to show it, on the record, where the record matters.

And there is more at stake in it than these programs. There is a huge opportunity here for the cloud companies and the frontier labs to deliver on the promises they keep making, and in doing so, to win back a country that is largely skeptical of technology, and of the data centers rising to power it. Skepticism like that is not answered with announcements. It is answered with results people can see: planes that fly, records that work, systems that hold up on the worst day, not the easiest one. Deliver that, on work the whole country depends on, and the trust follows. That is the opportunity in front of them.
